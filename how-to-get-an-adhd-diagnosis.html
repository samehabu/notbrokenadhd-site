Getting assessed

How to Get an ADHD Diagnosis as an Adult: Criteria and Steps

Suspecting you have ADHD and actually getting assessed can feel like two very different things — especially if you’ve been brushed off before. Here’s how the process really works, and how to walk in prepared.

Last updated: 30 September 2026

What an adult ADHD assessment involves

There is no single blood test or brain scan that diagnoses ADHD. A trained clinician makes the diagnosis clinically, by combining several things:

  • A structured interview about your symptoms, how long they’ve been present, and their impact across settings.
  • Your developmental history — signs must trace back to childhood, even if no one noticed then.
  • Validated rating scales (like the ASRS) to support, not replace, the interview.
  • Ruling out look-alikes — thyroid problems, sleep disorders, anxiety and depression can all mimic parts of ADHD.

ADHD diagnosis criteria for adults

Clinicians use the DSM-5-TR (or the closely related ICD-11). For adults (age 17 and over), all of these must be true:

  • At least five symptoms of inattention and/or of hyperactivity–impulsivity, for at least six months.
  • Several symptoms were present before age 12, even if nobody named them at the time.
  • Symptoms show up in two or more settings, such as work, home and relationships.
  • They clearly interfere with how you function day to day.
  • They are not better explained by another condition, such as anxiety, depression or a sleep disorder.

In plain terms, inattention symptoms include things like missing details, drifting off in conversations, struggling to finish or organise tasks, losing things and forgetting routine obligations. Hyperactivity–impulsivity symptoms include restlessness, difficulty staying seated, talking a lot, interrupting and struggling to wait. The result is described as predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive–impulsive, or combined.

Who can diagnose it

A diagnosis is made by a qualified clinician experienced with adult ADHD: usually a psychiatrist, and in some countries a licensed psychologist or specialist ADHD service. Your family doctor (GP) is often the first step and can refer you. Rating-scale apps and online quizzes can hint whether it is worth pursuing, but they cannot diagnose.

The assessment, step by step

  1. Self-screen. A validated screener such as the WHO's ASRS can suggest whether an assessment is worth seeking. It is a starting point for the conversation, not a diagnosis.
  2. First appointment. Book with your GP or primary care doctor, or go straight to a specialist where your health system allows it. In the US, check whether your insurance needs a referral.
  3. Get referred or book a specialist. In the UK, the NHS says waits for an adult assessment can be several months or years; in England, the Right to Choose scheme may let your GP refer you to a clinic with a shorter list. Private assessments are faster but cost money.
  4. The full assessment. A detailed clinical interview, rating scales, your childhood history, and often a conversation with a partner, parent or close friend. The clinician also rules out look-alike conditions.
  5. The result. You should receive a written report: either the diagnosis, or what better explains your difficulties. Ask for it and keep it.
  6. Next steps. A treatment plan: education about ADHD, therapy or coaching, workplace or study adjustments, and medication where appropriate, prescribed and reviewed by a specialist.

How to choose an assessor

Before you book, it is worth asking:

  • Do you assess adults, not only children?
  • How many sessions does the assessment take?
  • Do you use a structured interview (such as DIVA-5) and rating scales?
  • Will you speak to a family member or partner if needed?
  • Will I get a written report, and can you (or someone you work with) start treatment?
  • What does it cost, and is it covered by my insurance or health service?

How to prepare

  1. Bring evidence, not just feelings. A vague “I can’t focus” is easy to dismiss. Bring concrete examples from work, study, home and relationships.
  2. Show the timeline. Note that the difficulties have been present since childhood, and any family history of ADHD.
  3. Name the impact. Diagnosis rests on real impairment in more than one area of life — spell out missed deadlines, money trouble, or strained relationships.
  4. Ask directly. Say plainly: “I’d like to be assessed for ADHD,” or ask for a referral to someone who can.

What to bring

  • Old school reports or teacher comments, if you have them.
  • A short list of your current difficulties, with concrete examples.
  • Previous medical letters and a list of the medicines you take.
  • If possible, someone who knew you as a child, or a short written account from them.

If you’re dismissed

Being taken seriously can be the hardest step, particularly for women and for the quiet, inattentive type. You are allowed to seek a second opinion, ask for a specialist, request your records, and change providers. Persistence isn’t being difficult — it’s advocating for your own health.

After the diagnosis

A diagnosis is a beginning, not a label. It opens the door to treatment — medication, therapy, coaching and workplace accommodations — and to strategies built for how your brain actually works. Finding the right treatment often takes a few adjustments; that’s normal, not failure.

Common questions

How do I get tested for ADHD as an adult?

Start with your GP or a psychologist/psychiatrist. The assessment is a clinical interview about your symptoms, their history since childhood and their impact, supported by rating scales — there is no single lab test.

Can a normal doctor diagnose ADHD?

A GP can start the process and refer you, but the formal diagnosis is usually made by a psychiatrist, psychologist or specialist ADHD service.

What if my doctor doesn’t take me seriously?

Bring concrete examples and a childhood-to-now timeline, state the impact plainly, and ask directly for an assessment or referral. If you’re dismissed, seek a second opinion or a specialist.

Do I need proof from childhood?

Helpful but not mandatory. Old school reports or a parent’s recollection strengthen the picture, but a skilled clinician can still take a careful developmental history from you.

What are the criteria for ADHD in adults?

Under the DSM-5-TR, adults need at least five symptoms of inattention and/or hyperactivity–impulsivity for six months or more, several present before age 12, showing up in two or more settings, clearly affecting daily life, and not better explained by another condition.

Can an online test diagnose ADHD?

No. Online questionnaires are useful for screening, but a diagnosis requires a clinical assessment by a qualified professional.

How long is the wait for an adult ADHD assessment?

It varies widely. The NHS says UK waits can be several months or years; in England, Right to Choose may shorten this. Private assessments are usually quicker but are paid for.

Walk in prepared

You can’t control the outcome, but you can control how ready you are. Bring examples, a timeline and the impact — and don’t stop at the first “no.”
