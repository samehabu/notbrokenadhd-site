ADHD across a lifetime

ADHD in women and girls: why it’s so often missed

For decades, ADHD was pictured as a hyperactive little boy. That image left millions of women and girls undiagnosed for years — often until adulthood, and often after a child of theirs is diagnosed first. Here’s what actually happens, and why it’s missed.

Why it gets overlooked

ADHD is not more “male” — it just tends to look quieter in girls and women. The hyperactivity that catches a teacher’s eye is more often internal: a racing mind, restlessness, over-talking, or emotional intensity rather than climbing the furniture. The inattentive presentation — drifting focus, disorganisation, forgetfulness — is easy to read as “dreamy,” “scattered,” or “not trying hard enough.”

Many girls also learn to mask. They compensate with perfectionism, people-pleasing, and enormous effort, so their struggles stay hidden behind decent grades — while the exhaustion of holding it together goes unseen.

How it commonly shows up

  • Inattentive traits — losing track of tasks, missing details, chronic lateness, and a home or bag that feels permanently disorganised.
  • Emotional intensity — big, fast reactions; low frustration tolerance; and rejection sensitivity that makes criticism land hard.
  • Anxiety and low self-esteem — often the result of years of being called lazy or “too much,” not a separate personality flaw.
  • Hormonal swings — symptoms can intensify with the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and perimenopause, because estrogen affects dopamine.

Why the late diagnosis matters

Being missed has a cost: years of blaming yourself for things that were never a character defect. That’s why a late diagnosis is so often described as a relief — it reframes a lifetime of “what’s wrong with me?” into a clear, treatable explanation. It is never too late to be assessed, and getting the right support in adulthood genuinely changes daily life.

What to do next

  1. Track your own patterns. Note concrete examples across work, home and relationships — and how long they’ve been present (ideally since childhood).
  2. Ask specifically for an assessment. Bring your examples to a GP, psychologist or psychiatrist and ask to be assessed for ADHD, or for a referral.
  3. Don’t accept a quick “no.” If you’re dismissed, seek a second opinion or a specialist who assesses adult ADHD.
  4. Build scaffolding while you wait. External reminders, single-tasking, and routines help regardless of a formal diagnosis.

Common questions

Why is ADHD missed in women?

Because it often shows up as inattentiveness, internal restlessness and emotional intensity rather than obvious hyperactivity — and many girls mask their struggles with perfectionism and hard work, so it’s mistaken for anxiety or being “scattered.”

Can you develop ADHD as an adult woman?

No — ADHD begins in childhood, even if it’s only recognised later. What changes in adulthood is that the demands of work, money and family finally outstrip the coping strategies that used to hide it.

Do ADHD symptoms change with hormones?

Yes. Because estrogen influences dopamine, many women notice symptoms worsen premenstrually and around perimenopause. It’s worth discussing with your clinician.

Is it worth getting diagnosed as an adult?

Very often, yes. A diagnosis reframes years of self-blame, opens the door to treatment and accommodations, and — for many women — is a profound relief.

You were never the problem

If this feels familiar, it isn’t proof of a diagnosis — but it may be a good reason to seek an assessment. The quiet, capable, exhausted profile is exactly the one the old stereotype hid.
