Living with ADHD

How to thrive with ADHD at work

Work is where ADHD often bites hardest — deadlines, admin, meetings and long solo tasks. But with the right structure, the same brain that struggles with boredom can be brilliant under pressure. Here’s how to tilt the odds.

Why work is hard — and it isn’t your character

Modern work is built around exactly what ADHD finds difficult: sustained attention on low-interest tasks, long timelines with delayed rewards, and constant task-switching. The problem isn’t effort or intelligence — it’s that the job’s demands collide with how the ADHD brain regulates attention and motivation. Naming that is the first step; the rest is scaffolding.

Beat the starting wall

  1. Shrink the first step. Task initiation is the hardest moment. Make step one absurdly small — “open the document and write one line” — so stepping over is trivial.
  2. Work in short sprints. Timeboxing and the Pomodoro technique turn an intimidating block into a finite 25-minute effort.
  3. Use body doubling. Working alongside someone (in person or on a call) borrows their momentum and makes starting easier.
  4. Remove one point of friction before you begin — close the extra tabs, silence notifications, set out what you need.

Tame deadlines and admin

  • Externalise everything. Your working memory is unreliable, so don’t rely on it — one capture inbox for tasks, visible reminders for time-based things, and alarms for anything that matters.
  • Break big projects into visible, tiny milestones with their own mini-deadlines, so “finish the report” becomes a series of doable steps.
  • Fight time blindness with visible clocks and timers, and by over-estimating how long things take.

Design the environment

Willpower is finite; environment is permanent. Cut distractions with notifications off and phone away, single-task by closing extra tabs, use noise-cancelling headphones, and build routines and checklists for recurring work so you’re not deciding from scratch each time.

Ask for reasonable accommodations

In many places you have a right to reasonable adjustments. Helpful ones include a quiet space or noise-cancelling headphones, written instructions, flexible hours or deadline reminders, and clearly-scoped tasks. You don’t have to disclose more than you’re comfortable with — frame it around what helps you deliver.

Play to your strengths

ADHD isn’t only a set of challenges. Many people bring real strengths to work: creativity, fast problem-solving, energy in a crisis, and deep hyperfocus on engaging problems. Where you can, shape your role toward interest-rich, varied, high-impact work — that’s where an ADHD brain shines.

Common questions

How can I focus at work with ADHD?

Shrink the first step until starting is trivial, work in short timed sprints, single-task by closing extra tabs, silence notifications, and externalise tasks and deadlines instead of relying on memory.

Should I tell my employer I have ADHD?

That’s your choice. You’re not obliged to disclose a diagnosis, but sharing it can unlock reasonable accommodations. Many people frame the conversation around what helps them deliver, rather than the label.

What accommodations help with ADHD at work?

Common ones include quiet space or noise-cancelling headphones, written instructions, flexible hours, deadline reminders, and clearly-scoped tasks broken into smaller pieces.

Is hyperfocus a good thing at work?

It can be a superpower on engaging, high-reward tasks — just watch that it doesn’t swallow the whole day or crowd out urgent-but-boring work. Pair it with timers and breaks.

Structure beats willpower

You don’t out-discipline ADHD at work — you build systems around it. Start with one change from this page, not all of them at once.
